# Piano-learning apps: landscape and competitive overview

Research snapshot: **25 October 2026**. This is a general market overview for
Kidmidi, based on official product pages, help centers, app-store listings and
public repositories. It is a desk review, not a hands-on product test or a market
share ranking. Platform listings do not establish compatibility with a particular
physical keyboard.

Kidmidi's [product direction](product-direction.md) is a long-term, extensible MIT
platform with age-specific family play, teacher-informed folk/public-domain
repertoire, optional accounts and portable data. Read the comparisons below as
context for that direction.

## Quick read

- **Guided beginner courses:** Simply Piano, Skoove, Piano Academy and Yousician
  sell a learning path, feedback and a song catalog.
- **Learn a particular song:** flowkey combines pianist video with notation;
  Synthesia and La Touche Musicale emphasize visual play-along practice.
- **Notation and more deliberate practice:** Piano Marvel and Playground
  Sessions combine lessons with reading, exercises and performance feedback.
- **Open source exists, including new browser apps.** Dacapo is the most visibly
  active newcomer in this sample; Pianio is especially relevant to Kidmidi's
  notation and MIDI architecture. Melodino is a tiny, child-focused project.
  PianoBooster is an older desktop option. Sightread needs special attention:
  its old code is open source, but its announced full desktop app will not be.
- Among the commercial examples below, individual annual plans often land near
  **US$100–160/year**, while month-to-month plans often cost **US$18–30/month**.
  Free tiers, family plans, regional prices and promotions change the comparison.

“Simply Piano” and “flowkey” are likely the names behind “simple piano” and
“easy Flow.” “Easy Piano” is too ambiguous to identify as one specific product;
this overview covers the main adjacent products rather than guessing.

**My shortlist for further investigation:** Dacapo for current development and
practice design; Pianio for notation, content structure and an untimed mode;
Melodino for child-facing interaction ideas, after resolving its license
inconsistency if reuse is contemplated. None is established here as a complete,
maintained, cross-platform open-source replacement for Simply Piano.

## What this means for Kidmidi

These are product judgments drawn from the reviewed material and Kidmidi's
[current behavior](../README.md), rather than claims established by user testing.

**Waiting for the right note is already common.** flowkey, Synthesia, Dacapo,
Pianio and Melodino all describe versions of it. Kidmidi's stronger distinction is
the combination: very young children and parent co-play, large readable notation,
patient pitch-only song practice, local exploration/recording, and a separate
positive-reward note game. The opportunity is making the first few minutes
comfortable and understandable, rather than inventing a waiting mode.

**The commercial products sell much more content and a longer path.** Courses,
licensed popular songs, videos and progression are recurring themes. Kidmidi's
small repertoire and early curriculum remain a real gap for a family expecting
months of instruction. A focused introductory playground is the current starting
point; the long-term aim is to grow the open platform and curriculum through
family use, teacher advice and contributions.

**Native mobile support matters.** Simply Piano and Skoove demonstrate that a
website and web billing do not imply browser-based lessons. Kidmidi currently
supports Web MIDI in its documented browsers and has no supported iOS/Safari MIDI
path. Reaching iPad families with physical keyboards remains a concrete platform
gap; an installable website alone does not establish native MIDI compatibility.

**Microphone recognition and MIDI input solve different access problems.**
Commercial apps such as Simply Piano, flowkey, Skoove and Yousician advertise
listening to acoustic instruments as well as digital-keyboard workflows. Kidmidi
currently needs MIDI, screen keys or computer keys. MIDI gives discrete note
events, while microphone access broadens the instrument audience. This research
does not establish comparative recognition accuracy, and does not change
Kidmidi's no-audio-transcription scope.

**Local use and portable notation are worth preserving.** Dacapo and the smaller
open projects show interest in low-friction browser practice. MusicXML supports
deliberately engraved scores; a MIDI import is useful but does not guarantee
readable notation. Kidmidi can keep its local recordings and score exports while
learning from competitors' loops, separate-hand practice and clearer next steps.

**A large song library has continuing costs.** Playground's lifetime plan limits
included copyrighted-song access to two years, and flowkey separates classical/
public-domain content from its broader catalog. For Kidmidi, growing a carefully
chosen, appropriately licensed starter repertoire is a plausible next step without
immediately copying a subscription catalog model.

## Suggested comparison session

Try one representative from each group before deciding on new features:

1. **Simply Piano:** beginner onboarding and how a family is guided to the first song.
2. **flowkey:** waiting mode, pianist video, looping and notation readability.
3. **Piano Marvel:** reading progression and how feedback changes between learning
   and assessment.
4. **Dacapo and Pianio:** the closest open browser-based comparisons for real
   notation, MIDI and self-paced practice.

Use the same short melody, adult-and-child setup and physical keyboard. Compare
time to first successful note, help needed from a parent, screen readability,
response to wrong/held/repeated notes, and what remains available for free. This
would turn the desk review into evidence about the experience Kidmidi aims to offer.
